Start with three things.

Hue

The colour family: red, yellow, blue and so on.

Value

How light or dark it is. Different values help shapes stand apart.

Saturation

How bright or muted it is. A quiet colour can make a bright one stand out.

What about warm and cool colours?

Reds, oranges and yellows often feel warm. Blues and blue-greens often feel cool. But compare the colours beside each other: a reddish blue can feel warmer than a greenish blue. Warm colours do not always come forward; light, edges and surrounding colours matter too.

Choose colours that work together.

This is a traditional red–yellow–blue artist’s wheel, not a paint-mixing calculator. Pigments behave differently from screen colours.

Let one colour take up most of the space. Use another in smaller areas, and save your strongest contrast for the part you want people to notice.

Why don’t red and blue always make a bright purple?

The pigments matter. A red that leans towards orange can dull the mixture. Try a rose or magenta with ultramarine, then compare it with the reds and blues you already have. Start with small amounts and write down the paint names.

A traditional wheel helps you choose colours. It cannot promise a mixing result. Cyan, magenta and yellow are another useful starting set; no three paints can make every colour.

Tint, shade and tone — what do those words mean?

A tint is a colour mixed with white. A shade is mixed with black. A tone is often used for a colour mixed with grey. In watercolour, more water lets the white paper show through; that is different from adding white paint.

The colour beside it changes what you see.

Both centre squares are exactly the same grey. Try judging your colour beside its neighbours, not on its own.

What should I paint underneath?

“Rich” might mean glowing, deep or full of contrast. A good first layer depends on what you want the next layer to do.

How can I tell if my paint is transparent?

Look for a transparency rating on the tube or colour chart. You can also paint a thin stripe over a dry black line. If the line stays clear, the paint is fairly transparent. If it disappears, the paint is more opaque. Test the actual thickness you plan to use.

Glazing, scumbling and underpainting

Underpainting: an early painted layer that helps organise the picture. It comes after any needed surface preparation.

Glazing: a thin, see-through layer over dry paint. It changes the colour below without fully hiding it.

Scumbling: lightly dragging a small amount of opaque paint over a dry layer so the lower colour shows between the marks.

A primer or ground prepares the surface for paint. A coloured underpainting does not replace a primer.

Will the opposite colour make my paint richer?

Sometimes, but in different ways. Orange peeking between blue strokes can feel lively. A transparent blue layer covering orange may become darker or duller. Fully opaque blue hides the orange. Mixing, layering and placing colours side by side are three different actions.

Can I mix different kinds of paint in the layers?

Do not put acrylic over oil. Oil over acrylic needs a suitable, fully dried preparation and the paint maker’s guidance; a thick, glossy acrylic layer is not a good default. Traditional gouache and watercolour can re-wet. Acrylic gouache becomes water-resistant when dry. For your first test, keep the layers in the same paint system.

What isn’t working?

Small tests. Useful answers.

One colour, three bases

Try your top colour over white, a pale related colour and a muted opposite. Keep the paint, brush and thickness the same. Let each stage dry as needed. Which gives the result you want?

Make five steps

Make five values from light to dark. With watercolour, change how much water you use. With opaque paint, mix lighter and darker versions. Can you still tell the steps apart when you squint?

Mix less, learn more

Choose two paints. Mix a row from mostly one to mostly the other. Label the names and rough amounts. Try a second pair and compare the dried results.

Keep a useful test page

Write down the medium, surface, primer, pigment names, layer order and drying time. Leave half of each base uncovered. Compare under the same light once dry. A photo is useful, but the physical sample is your best colour reference.
